Enjin BahasaHow Malay is built

Grammar reference · Bahasa Melayu

Malay is a machine for making words

Learn a few hundred roots and a dozen affixes, and you can build and decode thousands of words you were never taught. This page is the engine room: the affixes, the sound rules, the sentence patterns, and the register shifts — with a builder that applies the rules for you.

1 · Roots and affixesWhy one word teaches you ten

Almost every Malay word is a root (kata dasar) wearing affixes. The root carries the meaning; the affixes say what role it plays — an action, a doer, a result, an abstract quality. Dictionaries list words under the root, so stripping affixes is also how you look things up.

RootThe family it grows

kerja workbekerja to work · mengerjakan work on sth · pekerja worker · pekerjaan occupation · kerjasama cooperation
tulis writemenulis to write · menuliskan write sth down for · penulis writer · tulisan writing, script · ditulis is written · bertulis bearing writing
besar bigmembesar grow bigger · membesarkan enlarge, raise a child · pembesar dignitary · kebesaran greatness; too big · terbesar biggest
satu onebersatu unite · menyatukan unify sth · kesatuan union · persatuan association · penyatuan unification · sesuatu something
guna usemenggunakan to use · berguna useful · pengguna user, consumer · penggunaan usage · kegunaan utility, purpose · digunakan is used

How to read an unknown wordPeel from the outside in. memperkenalkan → strip mem- and -kan → perkenal → strip per- → kenal (know, recognise). So: "cause someone to become acquainted" = to introduce.

3 · meN- and its sound changesThe single most important rule in Malay

meN- makes an active verb. The N changes shape to match the first sound of the root — and four letters disappear into it.

Root beginsPrefixExamples
l, m, n, r, w, y, ng, nyme-lihat → melihat · masak → memasak · rasa → merasa · nyanyi → menyanyi
b, f, vmem-baca → membaca · fitnah → memfitnah
p → dropsmem-pakai → memakai · potong → memotong · pukul → memukul
c, d, j, zmen-cari → mencari · dengar → mendengar · jual → menjual
t → dropsmen-tulis → menulis · tolong → menolong · tutup → menutup
vowels, g, h, khmeng-ambil → mengambil · guna → mengguna(kan) · hantar → menghantar
k → dropsmeng-kira → mengira · kirim → mengirim · kunci → mengunci
s → dropsmeny-sapu → menyapu · simpan → menyimpan · sewa → menyewa
one-syllable rootsmenge-cat → mengecat · bom → mengebom · pos → mengepos
The four that vanish: K · T · S · PRemember them as KiTa SePakat ("we agree"). They only vanish when a vowel follows: kritik → mengkritik, proses → memproses keep their consonant because a consonant follows.

Spoken Malay drops the prefix

In conversation the bare root is normal and sounds natural: Saya beli roti rather than Saya membeli roti. Keep meN- for writing, formal speech, and the news.

The contrast that mattersmasukkan moves a thing into something: Masukkan baju ke dalam beg. masuki takes the place as its object: Mereka memasuki bilik itu. Same root, two different objects.

Spellingber- becomes be- before a root starting with r (renang → berenang) and before a root whose first syllable ends in -er (kerja → bekerja, serta → beserta). One irregular to memorise: ajar → belajar.

6 · di- and the passiveMalay reaches for the passive far more than English

DoerPatternExample
he / she / they / unknowndi- + root (+ oleh X)Surat itu ditulis oleh Ali. — The letter was written by Ali.
I / you / wepronoun + bare rootSurat itu saya tulis. — The letter was written by me.
notices and signsdi- + rootDilarang merokok. No smoking. · Dibuka 9 pagi. Opens 9 a.m.
Never say thisSurat itu ditulis oleh saya. With I/you/we, the pronoun goes straight in front of the bare verb: Surat itu saya tulis. Likewise Kereta itu kami sewa, not disewa oleh kami.

Why so much passive?

Malay uses the passive to put the important thing first, the way English uses the topic position. News writing is full of it: Lima orang ditahan polis — five people (were) detained (by) police. When you read Malay news, expect di- on most main verbs.

The one word-order rule

The head word comes first; everything describing it follows. Read Malay noun phrases backwards into English:

MalayWord by wordEnglish
rumah besar ituhouse big thatthat big house
kereta merah sayacar red mymy red car
buku sejarah Malaysia yang barubook history Malaysia which newthe new Malaysian history book
pelajar universiti tahun akhirstudent university year finalfinal-year university student

yang — the hinge of complex sentences

yang turns anything into a modifier: orang yang tinggi (the tall person), buku yang saya beli (the book I bought), yang ini (this one), Apa yang berlaku? (What happened?). If a sentence stretches past a few words, yang is usually holding it together.

A reading routine that works1 — Find the main verb and check its prefix (meN- active, di- passive, ber- intransitive). 2 — Whatever sits before a di- verb or right after a meN- verb is the key participant. 3 — Strip affixes off any unknown word to find the root. 4 — Treat yang as an opening bracket: everything after it describes what came before.

17 · Traps for English and Arabic speakers

Don't invent a verb "to be"Saya adalah lapar. → Saya lapar. adalah/ialah belong to formal writing between two nouns, not before adjectives.
tidak vs bukanAdjectives and verbs take tidak; nouns take bukan. Dia tidak doktor → Dia bukan doktor.
kami vs kitaTelling your friend "let's go" with kami excludes them. Use kita when the listener is included.
Adjective orderEnglish puts the adjective first; Malay puts the noun first. besar rumah → rumah besar.
Passive by meditulis oleh saya → saya tulis.
Arabic interferenceMalay borrowed the words, not the grammar: no definite article, no dual, no gender agreement, no verb conjugation for person. kitab is a religious book (an ordinary one is buku), and sahabat means a close friend in everyday use.
Habits that make you sound localRepeat the key word instead of "yes". Use a kinship title (kak, bang, pak cik) instead of "you". Say tak apa often. Add -lah sparingly until you feel the rhythm. And greet with Dah makan? — "have you eaten?" is a real greeting, not an invitation.
